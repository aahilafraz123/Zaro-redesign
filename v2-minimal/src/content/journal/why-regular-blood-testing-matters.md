Most people walk out of an annual physical with one word in their heads: normal. It's a reassuring word, and usually an honest one. But it answers a narrower question than most people assume. "Normal" means nothing in that panel has crossed a clinical line yet. It doesn't mean nothing is moving.

## What a physical is built to do

A routine physical typically looks at somewhere between 12 and 15 markers: a basic metabolic panel, a blood count, a standard lipid panel, maybe glucose. Each one is read against a reference range, and the job of that range is to separate people who need treatment now from people who don't. That's a useful job. It's just a different job from noticing that something is drifting.

Most chronic conditions don't arrive on the day they're diagnosed. Insulin resistance, for example, usually builds for years before fasting glucose or HbA1c move enough to flag. Fasting insulin often rises first, sometimes up to a decade earlier. A panel that never measures fasting insulin can't see that early chapter, no matter how carefully it's read.

## One point can't show a direction

Even the markers a physical does measure have a second limitation: a single result tells you where a number is, not where it's going. An LDL of 125 looks the same on paper whether it was 95 two years ago or 160. Those are completely different stories. One is a number climbing toward trouble; the other is a number that's already responding to changes you've made.

That's why testing regularly matters more than testing exhaustively once. Two or three comparable results, a few months apart, turn isolated numbers into a direction you can act on.

## Earlier is cheaper, in every sense

The case for catching drift early isn't only medical. A borderline marker caught early usually calls for small, inexpensive changes: sleep, food, movement, sometimes a supplement. The same marker caught years later, after it has become a diagnosis, tends to mean medication, specialists and ongoing management. The information is the same; what changes is how much room you have left to respond to it.

## What regular testing looks like in practice

A sensible rhythm starts with one full baseline before you change anything, so there's a real starting point on record. From there, retesting every three to six months shows whether what you're doing is working, and the cadence can relax once things are stable.
